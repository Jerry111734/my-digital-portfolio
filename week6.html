Weekly reflection · WEEK 6

Identity, Algorithmic Identity and Representation

How data and algorithms create a version of who we are online.

What is algorithmic identity?

Week 6 asked how online platforms build pictures of us from the data we leave behind. Cheney-Lippold (2017) describes an algorithmic identity that can change as our activity changes. I used to think of online identity mainly as the profiles, photos and posts we choose to share. This reading made me consider the identity a platform creates about us, often without asking us directly.

For example, if I regularly watch Marvel-related videos, a platform may categorise me as a superhero fan. That may be accurate, but it is only one part of me. Watching something once does not necessarily mean that it represents my personality or beliefs.

Input and output: the data behind a profile

In the workshop, the first task was to look at what platforms collect, including posts, search history, location information, browsing activity and other kinds of data. The second task focused on the outputs: the advertising interests and user categories produced from these records.

What surprised me was the difference between an action and its interpretation. A platform can record a click or a search, but it cannot easily know the intention behind it. The advertising profile may be useful for marketing without being a complete or fair description of a person. This connects with Gillespie's (2014) argument that algorithms make choices about what becomes relevant and visible.

Process: thinking through Sumpter’s method

The practical task drew on Sumpter (2018). We were asked to select 32 social media friends, examine each person's most recent 15 posts, and place every post into one of 13 categories. This would mean 480 posts in total, followed by a chart comparing selected categories.

Looking at the method raised a problem for me: one post can contain several meanings, but the spreadsheet asks for a single category. A photograph with friends at a concert could be labelled friends, music or lifestyle. The researcher's judgement determines which part is counted. I would need clear coding rules and careful treatment of other people's privacy to use this method responsibly.

My reflection

This week changed the way I think about data. It is tempting to treat a label or chart as an objective description, but categories are created by people and applied through particular systems. Algorithms can identify patterns without knowing the full context of someone's life. I would therefore be careful about claiming that a collection of online posts represents someone's complete identity.

I want to examine how platforms create these labels, rather than simply accepting that the labels describe who I am.

References

  • Cheney-Lippold, J. (2017). We Are Data: Algorithms and the Making of Our Digital Selves. New York: NYU Press. Introduction.
  • Gillespie, T. (2014). 'The Relevance of Algorithms'. In T. Gillespie, P. Boczkowski and K. Foot (eds.), Media Technologies. Cambridge, MA: MIT Press.
  • Sumpter, D. (2018). Outnumbered: From Facebook and Google to Fake News and Filter-Bubbles – The Algorithms That Control Our Lives. London: Bloomsbury. Chapter 3.
