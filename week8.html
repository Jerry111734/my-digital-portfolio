Weekly reflection · WEEK 8

Digital Ecologies and Sensory Mapping

Listening to places, drawing sensory experiences and noticing more-than-human connections.

Digital ecologies: more than what we can see

Week 8 moved from online data to the relationship between people, technologies and the environments around us. We explored digital ecologies and more-than-human relationships: the idea that a place is shaped not only by people but also by materials, plants, animals, weather, infrastructure and devices.

The workshop encouraged us to pay attention to senses other than sight. Instead of simply looking at a building on a map, we were invited to think about how it sounds, feels and changes as we move through it.

Listening and sensory mapping

The activities were based around Roger Stevens and included sensory activation, a listening exercise outside, following a sensory trail, and creating a map that could communicate those experiences to others.

Schafer (1994) explains that a soundscape includes sounds we usually ignore as well as sounds that attract our attention. His distinction between keynote sounds, signals and soundmarks helped me think about what it means to listen to a place carefully rather than treating every sound as background noise.

Our group's sensory map

As part of the workshop, our group created the hand-drawn map below. We used repeated purple lines, orange and red arrows, dotted marks and different coloured symbols to represent a route and sensory impressions. Instead of trying to make a conventional map of corridors, we experimented with showing how a space might be experienced through sound.

Looking at our drawing, I can see both the strength and limitation of this approach. The patterns make the route feel active, but a person who was not in our group might not know what every symbol means. A short legend or a sound recording could help communicate those meanings more clearly. The map shows an interpretation of experience, not a complete record of every sound in the building.

How the readings connect

Schafer argues that recording technology does not give us the same overall impression of a soundscape that a photograph gives of a landscape. A microphone records selected sounds from a particular place and moment. This helped me understand why a sensory map can also be selective.

The Terra Forma approach to speculative mapping offered another way to challenge ordinary maps. Rather than treating space only as fixed locations and borders, the workshop reading encouraged us to notice relationships between living things, materials and environments. It made me think about how a drawing might represent connections and experiences, not just a route from one point to another.

Reflection and next step

My main takeaway is that there is no single neutral way to represent a place. A traditional map is useful for finding a location, but it does not tell us how a place sounds or feels. Our drawing makes some sensory features visible while leaving others unclear.

If I developed this activity further, I would add a simple legend and notes about the order of sounds along the route. I would also consider how someone who could not visit Roger Stevens might experience the map through an audio description. This would make our work easier to understand without pretending that it captures every person's experience.
