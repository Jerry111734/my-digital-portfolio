Weekly reflection · WEEK 7

Reflective Pause

Questioning data categories, research choices and the limits of AI detection.

Reflective pause

Week 7 returned to the questions from Sumpter's social media experiment. Instead of collecting more information, we were asked to think about the choices made during classification, what the results could show, and what they might leave out. This felt like an important pause after the more technical tasks in Week 6.

Can a spreadsheet represent a friend?

One issue I kept returning to was context. If someone shares a photo of a meal, I may put it in lifestyle. However, the photo might actually be about celebrating a family occasion or remembering a friend. A single category simplifies the post, and someone who knows the person might interpret it differently.

This made me question whether numbers always make our findings more accurate. A chart can clearly show how posts were classified, but it cannot explain why people posted them. If I repeated the exercise, I would first make the categories clearer, record uncertain cases, and explain how my own decisions affect the results.

Feedback, privacy and representation

The workshop also suggested an optional extension: asking five friends whether they recognised themselves in the data collected about them. I think this is a useful way to test a researcher's interpretation rather than assuming it is correct. However, it also raises questions about consent, privacy and how people may feel when shown a profile made from their posts.

This connects back to Cheney-Lippold's idea of algorithmic identity. Both a social media algorithm and a researcher can construct a simplified picture of a person. Neither picture should be treated as the whole person.

Assessment 1 and AI detection

Another part of the workshop involved thinking critically about AI detection and academic writing. The following week's materials revealed that the writing extracts used in the activity came from student work that had already received at least a 2:1 grade. That detail matters: a quick judgement about what sounds “AI-generated” is not the same as reliable evidence about how a text was produced.

For my own academic work, I think it is more useful to be clear about the writing process, sources and choices I make than to rely on how a piece of writing happens to sound.

My takeaway

This week reminded me that reflection is not just about saying whether an activity worked. It also means asking what was excluded, whose interpretation mattered, and how the method might be improved. I want my portfolio to show these questions, not only finished results.
