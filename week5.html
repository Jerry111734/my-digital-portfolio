Weekly reflection · WEEK 5

Data and Data Visualisation

Thinking critically about the journey from collecting data to communicating it visually.

What the workshop focused on

Week 5 explored how data visualisations are created and how they communicate information. We revisited key terms including datasets, variables and chart types. The workshop had two parts: reflecting on the dataset-generation task from Week 4, and considering how that data could be visualised.

Part A: Reflecting on generating a dataset

The first task asked us to think about what was challenging in the previous week's work, what our data could tell us, what we might do differently and how data collection relates to power.

Our group topic considered students' use of generative AI and questions of originality in arts and humanities work. We discussed using public online material from platforms such as Reddit, TikTok and Xiaohongshu. Looking back, I can see that choices about which platforms, posts and categories to include can strongly influence the picture a dataset presents. Online discussions do not represent every student equally, so I would need to explain the limits of any conclusions drawn from them.

Part B: From data to visualisation

The second part introduced ways to present data using charts, with Microsoft Excel as a starting point. The materials also suggested tools such as Tableau Public, Gephi and Flourish, and offered public datasets for further practice. Choosing a chart is not just a technical step: its type, labels and categories can shape how someone understands the results.

For our group's topic, a simple bar chart might help compare the frequency of clearly defined themes in a dataset. However, I would first need to check that the categories are consistent and that the data is suitable for comparison. Otherwise, a visually convincing chart could still communicate a misleading message.

Considering the audience

The workshop asked us to identify who would see our visualisation, what we wanted them to think, feel or do, and which messages they would need in order to understand it. For a project about generative AI and student originality, a possible audience would be other students and university staff. I would want to make different perspectives visible without suggesting that a small collection of online posts represents all students.

My reflection and next steps

The most useful lesson for me was that visualisation is also a form of storytelling. It can reveal patterns, but it can also hide uncertainty when the source, sample or categories are not explained. This connects with our earlier discussion of data ethics and power: the person who chooses what to count also influences what the audience gets to see.

My next step is to practise creating a clear chart from a suitable dataset in Excel, explain where the data came from, and check whether the labels and chart type support the intended message. I also want to be more explicit about the limitations of the data before drawing conclusions.
