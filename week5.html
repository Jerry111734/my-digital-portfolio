Weekly reflection · WEEK 5

Data and Data Visualisation

Thinking critically about the journey from collecting data to communicating it visually.

The two workshop tasks

Week 5 moved from collecting data to questioning what a dataset can show and how it might be visualised. Part A asked us to reflect on the Week 4 dataset-generation task: what was difficult, what our data could tell us, what we would change, and how data collection relates to power. Part B considered how data might be presented visually for a particular audience.

Part A: Revisiting the AI originality project

For our group topic, the first difficulty is deciding what counts as a relevant example of generative AI use. A comment about asking AI for ideas is different from one about submitting AI-generated text. If the categories are vague, two researchers could code similar posts differently.

Another limitation is the source. TikTok, Xiaohongshu and Reddit have different communities, and people who post publicly are not necessarily representative of students in arts and humanities. This makes it important to describe how posts would be selected, and to avoid generalising from a small or uneven collection of examples.

The question about power matters here: the people designing the categories decide which behaviours can be counted, while platform algorithms influence which discussions are easiest to find.

Part B: Planning a visualisation

We considered tools including Microsoft Excel, with Tableau Public, Gephi and Flourish mentioned as other possibilities. For this research question, one possible visualisation would be a bar chart comparing clearly defined types of AI use, such as brainstorming, drafting or editing. This is an example of a possible chart, not a claim that I created or analysed that dataset.

Before making any chart, I would need a consistent set of categories and an explanation of the sample size. A chart with polished colours can still be misleading if the underlying posts were collected unevenly.

Audience and message

A possible audience would be university students and tutors discussing originality and academic practice. I would want the visualisation to show that “using AI” is not a single behaviour, while making the limits of the evidence visible. Clear labels and a short explanation of the data source would matter as much as the chart type.

My reflection and next step

This week made me think about visualisation as an editorial decision, not just an Excel skill. Selecting a category, scale or label influences what people notice. I would next test a small, clearly described dataset, check the values and explain uncertainty before treating any pattern as a conclusion.

No completed dataset or chart is presented here; this entry documents the workshop questions and my proposed approach.

The examples above are plans and reflections. They are not reported findings from a finished dataset or visualisation.
